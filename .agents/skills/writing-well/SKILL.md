# Writing Well

Good prose gives its intended reader something useful: an explanation, an argument, a decision, or a memorable description. Editing should make that value easier to find without flattening the author's voice.

## Establish the point

Before drafting, answer:

```txt
Who will read this, and what do they already know?
What should they understand, believe, or do afterward?
Which evidence, mechanism, or example supports the point?
What tone and level of formality fit the setting?
```

If the source material cannot answer a factual question, ask for the missing information or mark the gap. Never manufacture a name, number, quotation, citation, or technical detail to make a rewrite sound concrete.

Readers care about consequences they recognize. Name the cost, conflict, limitation, or opportunity in terms used by the intended audience. Do not claim that something matters and leave the reason unstated.

```txt
weak:   This underscores the transformative importance of durable execution.
better: If step 4 fails, the workflow retries that step and keeps the earlier outputs.
```

Specificity is not mandatory decoration. Add detail when it supports the point; cut the claim when no supporting detail exists.

## Arrange information for flow

Readers use sentence position to work out how ideas connect. These tendencies are useful diagnostics, not fixed rules:

1. **Start from known context.** A sentence often reads more smoothly when its opening links to the previous sentence. Keep a stable subject across related sentences when possible.
2. **Put emphasis near the end.** New or important information often lands best in the stress position: the sentence end, or the words before a colon or semicolon.
3. **Keep subject and verb close.** Long interruptions between them hide the action. Move essential context into its own clause; cut inessential context.
4. **Put action in the verb.** Prefer “inhibits” to “provides inhibition of.” Noun-heavy constructions make readers reconstruct the event.
5. **Give context before details that depend on it.** Introduce the frame before asking the reader to interpret evidence inside it.

To diagnose a rough paragraph, list the first few words of each sentence. Abrupt subject changes often reveal missing links. Then inspect paragraph boundaries: each paragraph should develop the current question, answer one raised earlier, or establish the need for the next.

Do not force every sentence into the same pattern. Repetition, delayed context, passive voice, and abrupt transitions can all work when they serve emphasis, pacing, or a stable point of view. For example, passive voice may be clearest when the recipient of an action is the paragraph's subject.

## Use plain language without flattening the prose

Prefer familiar words and direct syntax when they express the meaning accurately. Keep technical terms that give a field precision. Replace needless abstraction, not specialized vocabulary.

Drafting and editing need different tolerances. A loose draft can expose the idea; a slower revision can remove repetition, repair logic, and sharpen wording. Read difficult passages aloud. Speech is not the standard for every genre, but an awkward spoken rhythm often reveals overloaded syntax.

Sentence length should follow the thought. Several short sentences can sound mechanical; several long ones can blur their hierarchy. Vary length when the material warrants it, not to manufacture drama.

## Match the register

- **Technical and scientific writing:** keep exact domain terms, identify evidence, and distinguish observation from inference. Use “we” for the authors' work when the venue permits it. Prefer named sources to “researchers have shown.”
- **Documentation:** lead with the user's task, required context, and observable result. Keep warnings specific. Do not turn every explanation into marketing copy.
- **Essays, blogs, and newsletters:** let voice and rhythm remain visible. Address the reader directly when natural, but avoid a rehearsed teaching patter such as “Let's unpack this.”
- **Product and launch copy:** state capability, audience, and constraint. Claims such as “fast,” “secure,” or “seamless” need evidence or a narrower formulation.

## Avoid slop

Treat these patterns as prompts to inspect a sentence, not as banned words. A phrase may be justified by evidence, genre, quotation, or deliberate rhythm. Do not replace a flagged word with a grander synonym.

### Unsupported importance

- **Inflated significance:** “transformative,” “pivotal,” “a testament to,” “paradigm shift,” or “far-reaching implications” without a stated change.
- **Grand openings and endings:** “In today's rapidly evolving landscape” and “Looking ahead, X will play a pivotal role.” Start or finish with the actual subject.
- **Magic modifiers:** “quietly,” “remarkably,” “deeply,” “fundamentally,” and similar words used to supply emotion or scale that the sentence has not shown.
- **Vague authority:** “studies show” or “experts believe” without an identifiable source.
- **False scope:** “always,” “never,” “everyone,” broad historical sweeps, or ranges whose endpoints merely suggest breadth.

Fix: state what changed, for whom, under what conditions, and by how much if the source provides that information. Otherwise narrow or remove the claim.

### Indirect language

- **Noun-heavy action:** “provides an evaluation of” instead of “evaluates.”
- **Copula avoidance:** “serves as,” “stands as,” “features,” “boasts,” or “represents” used to dodge a clearer verb or plain “is.”
- **Empty trailing clauses:** “highlighting its importance,” “underscoring the need,” or “reflecting broader trends” when no consequence follows.
- **Prestige vocabulary:** “delve,” “realm,” “tapestry,” “leverage,” “foster,” “robust,” and “seamless” when a more exact term is available.
- **Stacked hedges:** “could potentially,” “may eventually,” or “might ultimately.” Keep the degree of uncertainty the evidence requires.

Fix: identify the actor and action. Use the field's precise term when it is more accurate than plain speech.

### Manufactured rhetoric

- **Formulaic contrast:** “It's not X, it's Y” and “not just X, but Y” when Y can stand alone.
- **Negative runway:** “Not X. Not Y. Z.”
- **Self-posed questions:** “Why does this matter?” followed immediately by the answer.
- **Dramatic fragments:** “The result? Devastating.” or “Clarity. That's the goal.”
- **Rhetorical setup:** “What if…?”, “Here's what I mean,” “Think about it,” or “At its core.”
- **Invented labels:** naming an ordinary tradeoff “the calibration paradox” or “the supervision trap” without analytical need.
- **Decorative punctuation:** em dashes, colons, or one-line paragraphs used mainly to simulate emphasis.
- **Excessive bicolons:** “Tests assert behavior. Review protects taste.”

Fix: state the claim directly. Keep rhetorical form when it creates a real contrast, controls pace, or suits the author's established voice.

### Repetition and templates

- **Throat-clearing:** “It's worth noting,” “Let me be clear,” and other phrases that delay the sentence's content.
- **Fractal summaries:** previewing the point, making it, and summarizing it again.
- **Synonym cycling:** changing “developers” to “engineers” to “practitioners” for the same group. Repeat the clearest term.
- **Historical stacking:** strings of famous names or examples that substitute for examining one case.
- **Template structure:** identical paragraph lengths, bold-label bullets throughout, rule-of-three lists, or tables that add no useful comparison.

Fix: keep the strongest version of the point. Vary structure in response to content, not to hide a template.

## Test uncertain passages

**Flatten test:** remove the line's rhythm and emphasis while preserving its claim. If little remains beyond “this matters,” add a supported mechanism or cut the line.

**Relation test:** state the link between adjacent ideas with a word such as *because*, *although*, *when*, or *therefore*. If no accurate connective fits, the draft may be implying a relation it has not established.

**Substitution test:** ask whether the sentence could be pasted into an unrelated piece after changing one noun. If so, it probably needs a detail from this subject or does not belong.

## Examples

```txt
NOUN-HEAVY ACTION → DIRECT VERB
before: The availability of TFIIIA provides a limitation on transcription in the egg extract.
after:  In the egg extract, the availability of TFIIIA limits transcription.
why:    The action moves from “limitation” to “limits”; the experimental frame comes first.

ABSTRACT VERBS → PLAIN SPEECH
before: We endeavor to facilitate the optimization of your onboarding journey.
after:  We help you set up new users faster.
why:    One direct verb replaces several abstractions.

CLAIM OF IMPORTANCE → FUNCTION
before: The scheduler serves as a robust foundation for reliable job processing, improving resilience.
after:  The scheduler records each completed step. After a crash, it resumes at the first unfinished step instead of running the whole job again.
why:    The revision states the recovery behavior rather than praising it.

INVENTED LABEL → MECHANISM
before: This creates the “calibration paradox,” reflecting the tension between aggregation and granularity.
after:  Calibration on aggregated results does not guarantee calibration within each subgroup.
why:    The revision names the limit without branding it.

RHETORICAL RUNWAY → DIRECT SCENARIO
before: What if your pipeline failed in production? The result? Wasted compute. The worst part? Nobody checks intermediate outputs.
after:  When the pipeline fails after alignment, it discards the intermediate files and starts again from raw reads.
why:    A concrete failure replaces staged questions and vague consequences.

OVER-CORRECTION → KEEP THE PRECISE TERM
before: The posterior predictive check showed systematic underdispersion.
bad edit: The model's guesses did not spread out enough.
keep:   The posterior predictive check showed systematic underdispersion.
why:    The technical terms are concise and exact for a statistical audience.
```

Examples demonstrate edits, not facts to copy into another draft. Preserve source meaning; do not add mechanisms the source does not establish.

## Editing process

1. Identify reader, purpose, genre, and desired effect.
2. Mark the draft's main claim and its support. Note unsupported claims and missing facts.
3. Repair argument and paragraph order before polishing sentences.
4. Improve sentence flow: topic continuity, context, subject–verb distance, and emphasis.
5. Cut repetition, empty setup, and unsupported rhetoric.
6. Restore useful specifics and preserve deliberate voice, terminology, uncertainty, and rhythm.
7. Read the revision as a whole. Check that local improvements did not create monotony or change the meaning.

## Review output

When critiquing rather than rewriting, prioritize consequential issues. Quote the passage, name the problem in plain language, and offer a revision when the source supports one. Use `ask` when a sound revision needs missing facts.

```txt
Verdict:  keep / revise / ask / cut
Passage:  quoted text
Problem:  effect on meaning, flow, tone, or credibility
Rewrite:  proposed wording, or the fact needed before rewriting
```

Do not report every detector hit. Group repeated problems, distinguish preferences from errors, and explain what a revision improves. Run the same checks on the rewritten prose before returning it.
